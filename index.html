Two funds, one market, half a million apart

Two savers pick index funds the same week, put the same $600,000 to work, and earn the same market return for the next twenty-five years. One holds a fund charging 0.03 percent a year. The other holds a "cheap" active fund charging 0.75 percent. Same market, same deposits, and the gap between them at the finish line is $502,377. Nobody wrote a bad check. The fee did it quietly, once a year, every year.

The first year the gap is $4,320: the 0.03 percent fund charges $180, the 0.75 percent fund charges $4,500. Both accounts then compound on what is left, so the expensive fund loses twice. It pays the fee, and it also forfeits twenty-five years of growth on every dollar the fee ate. Year one's $4,320 shortfall compounds to $18,509 by the end. A fee is never one charge. It is a charge plus every dollar that charge would have earned.

The math runs in monthly terms, since contributions land monthly. The 0.03 percent fund compounds at a 0.581 percent monthly rate; the 0.75 percent fund at 0.521 percent. Twenty-five years of those two rates turns $600,000 into $3,233,711 and $2,731,333 respectively. The gap is 83.7 percent of the original stake, which is why the standard "expenses wash out over time" line deserves a second look. Compounding does not average the drag out. It preserves it.

The saver who adds $500 a month to the cheap fund ends at $606,387. The saver who adds $500 a month to the expensive one ends at $526,960. Same $180,000 deposited over thirty years. The difference is $79,427, and the more expensive fund keeps about 87 cents of each dollar its cheaper rival keeps. Eighty-seven cents of every dollar of compounding still arrives. It just belongs to someone else.

Checking what a fund actually costs against its Firenomics breakdown of index fund fees is the habit that catches this early, and the site's withdrawal strategy guides extend the same ledger into retirement. The expensive fund does not have to be bad at its job to lose this race. It only has to be 0.72 percentage points more expensive, and the twenty-fifth year closes half a million behind.

Two funds, one market. The fee decides the finish line.
